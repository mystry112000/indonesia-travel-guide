Indonesia Packing List

Essential travel gear & what to wear

Packing Guide • 8 min read

Packing for Indonesia is different from packing for Europe or Japan. The climate is hot and humid year-round, but you'll encounter everything from air-conditioned malls to mossy jungle temples and active volcanoes. Pack too heavy and you'll regret it on every ferry crossing. Pack too light and you'll be buying overpriced sunscreen at the resort. This guide covers exactly what to bring for a balanced, comfortable trip across the archipelago.

The Golden Rule

Pack for layers, not outfits. Indonesia's weather is 28°C and humid 90% of the time, but temples require covered knees and shoulders, high-altitude treks get chilly, and air-conditioned transport is freezing. A sarong + light jacket solve almost every situation.

Clothing Basics

The Indonesian climate demands lightweight, breathable fabrics that dry quickly. Forget cotton if you sweat heavily — synthetic blends or bamboo fibres are better. Here is your core wardrobe:

  • 👕T-shirts (4-5): Light cotton or quick-dry. Loose fits breathe better in humidity.
  • 🩳Shorts (2-3): Above-knee is fine for beaches, but bring one pair of knee-length shorts for casual city wear.
  • 👖Long pants (1-2): Lightweight linen or trekking trousers. Essential for evenings, mosquitos, and temple visits.
  • 🧣Sarong (1): Non-negotiable. You will need it for temple entry. Men included.
  • 🧥Rain jacket / packable anorak: Sudden tropical downpours are a daily reality in wet season (Nov–Mar).
  • 🩴Flip-flops: For beaches, hostels, and quick trips to the warung.
  • 👟Walking shoes / trainers: For city exploring, day hikes, and non-sandy days.
  • 🧦Socks (3-4 pairs): Thin merino or synthetic. Do not wear cotton socks with trainers in humidity.

Pro Tip: Laundry

Laundry services are everywhere and cheap ($1-3 per kilo). You do not need a fresh outfit for every day. Pack for 4-5 days and wash along the way.

Temple & Modesty Wear

Indonesia is a Muslim-majority country with strong Hindu traditions in Bali. Temple dress codes are strictly enforced at major sites. Here is what you need:

  • 🧣Sarong: Required at every Hindu temple in Bali. Men must wear them too. Most temples offer rentals ($1), but owning your own is more hygienic and looks better in photos.
  • 👚Shawl or light scarf: Covers bare shoulders. Useful as a sun wrap, head covering for mosques, or makeshift towel.
  • 👗Long skirt or wide-leg pants: Must cover the knee at minimum. Ankle-length is preferred at major temples like Uluwatu and Besakih.

What NOT to wear at temples: Singlets, shorts above the knee, transparent clothing, hats inside temple grounds, shoes (remove before entering any temple building). Touching temple statues or climbing on shrines is considered deeply disrespectful regardless of what you are wearing.

Where to Buy a Sarong

Buy one on arrival for $3-5 at any market in Bali. Ubud Market and Sukawati Market have the best selection. You can also find batik sarongs in Java for $4-8. Skip the tourist stalls right outside temple gates — they charge double.

For mosques (especially in Java, Sumatra, and Lombok): Women must cover their hair, arms, and legs. Men should wear long pants and cover their shoulders. Most mosques provide robes and headscarves at the entrance. Dress conservatively in Aceh and West Sumatra — these are Indonesia's most religiously conservative regions.

Jungle Trekking Gear

Indonesia has world-class trekking: Mount Batur sunrise, Mount Rinjani crater lake, the Ijen blue flame, and the jungles of Sumatra where orangutans swing overhead. Here is what you need for serious trails:

  • 🥾Hiking boots or sturdy trail shoes: Waterproof is ideal. The trails can be muddy, slippery, and steep. Decathlon sells affordable options in major cities if you do not want to bring your own.
  • 👖Long trekking pants: Non-negotiable for leech protection. Tuck pants into socks in leech-heavy areas (Rinjani, Gunung Leuser).
  • 👕Quick-dry shirts (2): Look for UPF-rated hiking shirts. Cotton is dangerous on multi-day treks — once wet, it stays wet and causes chafing.
  • 🌧️Rain poncho: More practical than an umbrella on jungle trails. A cheap $3 poncho from a convenience store works fine, but a proper one will survive multiple treks.
  • 🧦Leech socks: Essential for Rinjani and Sumatra jungle treks. Buy them locally in Kuta Lombok or Medan for $2-3. They look silly but leeches cannot get through them.
  • 🧴Insect repellent (DEET 30%+): Dengue fever is a real risk. Apply before every trek. Repel or OFF! brands are widely available in Indonesia too.
  • 💧Reusable water bottle (1L+): Bring a stainless steel or hard plastic bottle. Many homestays and trek operators offer free filtered water refills. Avoid single-use plastic.

Trekking at Altitude

Mount Rinjani (3,726m) and Mount Semeru (3,676m) get cold at the summit — as low as 5–10°C. Pack a lightweight down jacket or fleece if you plan to summit. Your guide will provide sleeping bags, but a thermal base layer is worth bringing.

Beach Essentials

With over 17,000 islands, you will be spending time on the water. Whether you are surfing in Lombok, snorkelling in Raja Ampat, or lounging in the Gili Islands, these items make all the difference:

  • 🧴Reef-safe sunscreen (SPF 50): Indonesia's coral reefs are bleaching rapidly. Regular sunscreen containing oxybenzone and octinoxate is banned in marine parks. Bring biodegradable, reef-safe zinc sunscreen. Better yet, wear a rash guard and use less sunscreen.
  • 👕Rash guard / UV-protection swim shirt: Single best beach purchase. Prevents sunburn, protects from jellyfish stings, and you do not need to reapply sunscreen every 30 minutes.
  • 🤿Snorkel mask (dry-top): Rental masks are often scratched, leaky, and not sanitised properly. A decent mask costs $20-30 and transforms your snorkelling experience. Bring your own snorkel if you have space.
  • 📱Waterproof phone pouch: $5 on Shopee or Amazon. Essential for boat days, beach selfies, and protecting your phone from sudden rain.
  • 🪥Microfibre towel: Quick-drying, packs small. Most hostels and budget hotels charge $1-2 for towel rental. A microfibre towel pays for itself in a week.
  • 🩱Swimwear (2 sets): Humidity means swimwear takes 12+ hours to dry. Having a dry second set is not a luxury.

Beach Safety

Rip currents are common on Bali's west coast beaches (Kuta, Legian, Canggu). Always swim between the flags. Do not swim at beaches with a red flag — drownings are unfortunately common. The dry season (Apr–Oct) has calmer seas overall.

Tech & Gadgets

Staying connected in Indonesia is easy and cheap. Power outages happen in remote areas, but overall the tech infrastructure is solid. Here is what to bring and what to buy locally:

  • 🔌Universal power adapter: Indonesia uses Type C (Europlug), Type F (Schuko), and Type G (UK-style in some places). A universal adapter with USB ports covers everything.
  • 🔋Power bank (10,000mAh minimum): Essential for long ferries, overnight trains, and multi-day treks. Bring 20,000mAh if you rely on your phone for navigation and photos.
  • 🗺️Offline maps (Google Maps or Maps.me): Download the entire region on WiFi before you arrive. Mobile data is cheap but coverage drops in rural areas. Maps.me allows offline navigation with GPS only.
  • 📶eSIM or local SIM card: Telkomsel has the best coverage across the archipelago. A local SIM costs $2-3 with 10-30GB data. eSIM options like Airalo work instantly but cost slightly more per GB.
  • 📸GoPro / action camera / underwater camera: Snorkelling, waterfalls, and volcano sunsets deserve more than a phone camera. Even a $100 action camera beats a phone in waterproof scenarios.
  • 🔒VPN: Not strictly necessary for most travellers, but useful if you need to access banking apps or social media on shared WiFi. ExpressVPN, NordVPN, or Surfshark all work well.

Charging Tip

Voltage is 230V / 50Hz (same as Europe/Australia). Most modern chargers handle 100-240V, so you likely do not need a voltage converter — just a plug adapter. Check your charger brick for "100-240V" to confirm.

Health & Safety

Indonesia is a safe destination for travellers, but the tropical environment presents unique health challenges. Preparation is straightforward:

  • 🩹First aid kit: Plasters, antiseptic wipes, sterile gauze, tweezers (for splinters and leeches), bandages, and medical tape. Add blister plasters (Compeed) — your feet will thank you.
  • 💊Imodium or oral rehydration salts: Bali Belly (traveller's diarrhoea) affects most visitors at some point. Your gut is not used to the local bacteria. Pack loperamide for emergencies and ORS for recovery.
  • 🧴Hand sanitiser: Many warungs and street food stalls do not have hand-washing facilities. Carry a small bottle and use it before every meal.
  • 🦟Mosquito repellent: Already mentioned for treks, but worth repeating. Dengue, chikungunya, and malaria are present in parts of Indonesia. Apply repellent at dawn and dusk especially.
  • 🧊After-bite cream: Even with repellent, you will get bitten. Antihistamine cream (e.g. hydrocortisone 1%) stops the itching fast.
  • 💊Basic medications: Paracetamol, ibuprofen, antihistamines, motion sickness pills (for ferries). Pharmacies (apotek) are well-stocked in cities but limited in remote islands.

Vaccinations & Medical

Consult your doctor 4-6 weeks before travel. Recommended: Hepatitis A, Typhoid, Tetanus. Consider: Rabies vaccine (if trekking near monkeys). Malaria prophylaxis for Raja Ampat, Papua, and remote parts of Flores and Sumba. Travel insurance is non-negotiable — make sure it covers evacuation.

The One Thing Everyone Forgets

You have packed your clothes, your charger, your sunscreen. But there are a few items that travellers consistently leave behind — and end up needing on day one:

  • 🛍️Reusable shopping bag: Bali banned single-use plastic bags in 2019. Most shops charge $0.10-0.30 for a paper bag. A foldable nylon tote bag takes zero space and saves the hassle.
  • 🪥Small hand towel / face cloth: Many homestays and budget hotels do not provide face towels. A small quick-dry cloth is useful as a yoga mat cover, sweat towel, or makeshift napkin.
  • 👂Earplugs: Indonesian guesthouses can be noisy. Roosters crow at 4am. Mosques broadcast the adhan (call to prayer) at 4:30am. Scooters rev past at all hours. Foam earplugs weigh nothing and save your sleep.
  • 🧵Clothesline / travel washing line: A retractable cord with hooks costs $3 and lets you dry hand-washed clothes anywhere. Hotel balconies rarely have washing lines.
  • 🔒Padlock: Most hostel lockers and homestay room doors use a padlock. Bring a TSA-approved combination lock so you do not lose a key.
  • 🩴Flip-flops for the shower: A dedicated pair of rubber flip-flops for hostel showers prevents fungal infections. Do not use your going-out flip-flops for the bathroom.

The Ultimate Underrated Item

A headlamp or small LED torch. Power outages happen, especially in rural areas and during the wet season. A headlamp frees your hands for cooking, reading, or navigating dark temple paths at sunrise.

What NOT to Pack

Some items are not worth the suitcase space. Others are actively risky to bring into Indonesia. Here is what to leave at home:

  • 👖Heavy jeans: Denim takes 24+ hours to dry in humidity, weighs a ton, and is too hot for daytime exploring. Pack one pair of lightweight trousers instead.
  • 💍Expensive jewellery: Flashy items attract unwanted attention and are inappropriate at temples. Leave the Rolex at home. A simple wedding band is fine.
  • 📸Drones: Drones require a permit from the Ministry of Defence. Flying without one can result in confiscation and a fine. Even with a permit, many national parks and temple areas ban them.
  • 🚬Illegal drugs: Indonesia has the death penalty for drug trafficking. Possession of even small amounts can result in decades in prison. Do not risk it, period.
  • 📰Political or religious materials: Distributing religious tracts or political pamphlets is illegal and culturally insensitive. Do not bring materials that could be seen as proselytising.
  • 🔪Knives or multi-tools with blades: Strictly controlled. Leave your Swiss Army knife at home or pack it in checked luggage only.
  • 🧥Bulky winter coats: Unless you are climbing Carstensz Pyramid (4,884m) in Papua, you will not need anything thicker than a fleece. Indonesia is on the equator.

Luggage Recommendations

The right bag makes or breaks your trip. Indonesia's travel style involves lots of short hops — ferries, minibuses, becaks, and walking through narrow market alleys. Your luggage strategy matters:

  • 🎒40-50L backpack: Ideal for island-hopping itineraries (Gili Islands, Flores, Raja Ampat). Backpacks fit under bus seats, on scooter footboards, and in ferry holds. Osprey Farpoint 40 and Deuter 45+10 are popular choices.
  • 🧳Suitcase (carry-on size): Fine for resort-based travel with private transfers. Stick to 7-10kg carry-on size. Hard-shell suitcases do not handle ferry docks well.
  • 🎒Daypack (15-25L): Essential for temple visits, day hikes, and market shopping. Look for one with a water bottle pocket and anti-theft features. A foldable daypack that stuffs into its own pocket is a smart backup.
  • 📦Packing cubes (3-4): Game changer for organisation. Compress clothes, separate clean from dirty, and make unpacking at a new guesthouse a 30-second job. Recommended: Eagle Creek or cheap Amazon Basics.
  • 👜Dry bag (10L+): Protects electronics and documents on boat crossings. Ferries to the Gili Islands and Komodo are notorious for spray soaking backpacks.

Weight Limits

Domestic flights (Lion Air, AirAsia, Garuda) have strict carry-on limits of 7kg. Checked bags for short hops cost $5-15 extra. If you are backpacking, keep your main bag under 10kg so you can avoid checked luggage fees entirely.
