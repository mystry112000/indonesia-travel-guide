What to Eat in Indonesia

A food lover's guide to the archipelago's best dishes

What to Eat in Indonesia — A Food Lover's Guide

Published June 2026 • 8 min read

Indonesian cuisine is one of the world's most exciting — bold, aromatic, and incredibly diverse. With 17,000+ islands and 300+ ethnic groups, every region has its own specialties. This guide covers the must-try dishes and where to find them.

Nasi Goreng $1–3

Indonesia's national dish — nasi goreng (fried rice) is everywhere from five-star hotels to roadside carts. The magic is in the kecap manis, a sweet soy sauce that gives the rice its dark caramel colour and umami depth. It's typically topped with a fried egg (nasi goreng telur), prawn crackers (kerupuk), and pickled cucumbers (acar).

The dish has roots in Chinese fried rice, adapted over centuries to Indonesian tastes. Street vendors (kaki lima) cook it over blazing woks at dawn, infusing it with smoky wok hei. Regional variations include Nasi Goreng Jawa (drier, with more chilli), Nasi Goreng Kambing (goat meat, popular in Jakarta), and Nasi Goreng Aceh (spicier, with curry flavours).

Where to try it: Any local warung will serve a killer plate for under $2. In Bali, try Warung Nasi Goreng in Seminyak. For an upscale version, head to Nusa Dua resort restaurants. Tip: order it "dengan telur ceplok" (with a fried egg on top).

Nasi Goreng — Quick Tips

  • Price: $1–3 at street stalls, $4–7 at restaurants
  • Best time: Breakfast or late-night — it's eaten any time of day
  • Veg friendly: Ask for sayur (vegetable) version, most vendors can skip the meat
  • Spice level: Mild to medium — ask for sambal on the side to adjust

Rendang $2–5

Hailing from the Minangkabau culture of West Sumatra, rendang is a slow-cooked beef curry that CNN named #1 World's Most Delicious Food in 2017 — and for good reason. Chunks of beef are simmered for hours in coconut milk, lemongrass, galangal, turmeric, ginger, chilli, and a dozen other spices until the liquid evaporates and the meat is coated in a thick, caramelised paste.

The cooking process is a labour of love. Authentic rendang takes 4–6 hours of constant stirring. The result is impossibly tender beef with layers of heat, sweetness, and savoury depth. It keeps for weeks without refrigeration, which made it a staple for Minangkabau travellers. Dry rendang (rendang kering) is even chewier and more concentrated in flavour.

Where to try it: The gold standard is in Padang, West Sumatra, at places like Restoran Sederhana or Pagiyiki. Outside Sumatra, every Nasi Padang restaurant in Jakarta, Bandung, or Bali serves it. Look for the stack of plates in the window — that's your sign. Tip: rendang tastes even better the next day, so order extra to take away.

Rendang — Quick Tips

  • Price: $2–5 per portion at Padang restaurants
  • Best paired with: Steamed rice (nasi putih) and sayur nangka (young jackfruit curry)
  • Chicken version: Rendang ayam is also common — less fatty but just as flavourful
  • Vegan option: Rendang tempe or rendang jengkol (a polarising bean) — available in Padang restaurants

Sate (Satay) $1–3

Grilled meat skewers served with peanut sauce — sate is Indonesia's answer to BBQ. Marinated meat (chicken, goat, lamb, or even pork in non-Muslim areas) is skewered on bamboo sticks, grilled over charcoal until smoky, and served with a rich peanut dipping sauce, rice cakes (lontong), and raw shallots.

Regional variations are vast:

  • Sate Madura (East Java) — The most famous. Chicken or goat with a thick, sweet peanut sauce. The sauce is ground with garlic, chilli, and kecap manis.
  • Sate Padang (West Sumatra) — Beef or offal skewers with a thick yellow curry-like sauce made from rice flour and turmeric. No peanuts. Much spicier.
  • Sate Lilit (Bali) — Minced fish, chicken, or pork mixed with grated coconut, lemongrass, and spices, wrapped around a lemongrass stalk (not a bamboo stick). The lemongrass infuses the meat as it grills.
  • Sate Babi (Bali / Chinese Indonesian) — Pork skewers, popular in Bali and among Indonesia's Chinese community. Often marinated in honey and kecap manis.

Where to try it: For Sate Madura, head to Madura Island or any street vendor in Surabaya. For Sate Lilit, try Bumbu Bali restaurant in Nusa Dua. For Sate Padang, look for any Padang restaurant in Jakarta. The best sate is always from street vendors — look for the charcoal grill and the wispy smoke.

Nasi Padang $2–5

Nasi Padang is not a dish — it's an experience. Originating from the Minangkabau people of West Sumatra, it's a spread of 10–15 pre-cooked dishes served with steamed rice. The iconic presentation: small plates stacked on your table, sometimes covering the entire surface. You only pay for what you eat.

The system works like this: You sit down, the waiter places several plates in front of you — rendang, ayam pop (fried chicken), dendeng balado (crispy beef in chilli), gulai nangka (jackfruit curry), sambal lado (green chilli sambal), and more. You eat what you want. The waiter keeps a mental tally. At the end, you pay for the plates you touched. If you don't touch a dish, you don't pay for it.

Must-try dishes within Nasi Padang:

  • Rendang — The signature beef curry (see above)
  • Ayam Pop — Boiled then fried chicken, pale in colour but bursting with coconut flavour
  • Dendeng Balado — Thinly sliced beef, deep fried, tossed in a spicy chilli and tomato sauce
  • Gulai Tunjang — Cow tendon in rich coconut curry — melt-in-your-mouth texture
  • Sambal Lado Muda — A fiery green chilli sambal that accompanies everything
  • Telur Dadar Padang — Thick, crispy fried egg with herbs — incredibly satisfying

Where to try it: Restoran Sederhana (nationwide chain) or Garuda in Jakarta. For the real deal, visit Padang itself — the Nasi Padang stalls near Pasar Raya Padang are legendary.

Nasi Padang — Pro Tips

  • Don't be shy — ask for sambal hijau (green sambal) on the side, it's usually free
  • Eat with your right hand (makan pakai tangan) — it's the traditional way and enhances the flavour
  • Water is usually self-serve and free
  • Expect to pay $2–5 depending on how many dishes you eat

Balinese Food $1–5

Balinese cuisine is distinct from the rest of Indonesia, heavily influenced by Hinduism (pork is common) and the island's abundance of fresh spices and seafood. Meals are balanced around the concept of panca rasa — five tastes: sweet, sour, salty, bitter, and umami.

  • Babi Guling — Suckling pig stuffed with a spice paste of turmeric, coriander, lemongrass, and chilli, then spit-roasted for hours until the skin is impossibly crispy. The ultimate Balinese feast. Try it at Warung Babi Guling Ibu Oka in Ubud ($3–5).
  • Bebek Betutu — Duck (or chicken) stuffed with a powerful spice paste (base genep), wrapped in banana leaves and coconut husks, then slow-cooked for 6–8 hours. The meat falls off the bone. Find it at Bebek Bengil ("Dirty Duck Diner") in Ubud ($5–8).
  • Lawar — A traditional mix of minced meat (pork or chicken), grated coconut, herbs, and spices. Lawar merah includes pig's blood for colour — it's more delicious than it sounds ($1–2).
  • Sate Lilit — Already mentioned above, but worth repeating. The lemongrass skewers give it a unique citrus aroma ($1–2).
  • Tipat Cantok — A Balinese take on gado-gado: rice cake cubes, blanched vegetables, tofu, and peanut sauce ($1).

Where to eat Balinese food: Ubud is the culinary heart of Bali. Join a Balinese cooking class ($25–35) to learn the spice pastes. For the most authentic experience, attend a local temple ceremony where food is served family-style — ask your homestay host.

Javanese Food $1–4

Java is Indonesia's culinary powerhouse, with two distinct traditions: Central Javanese (sweet, influenced by royal courts of Yogyakarta and Surakarta) and East Javanese (savoury and spicy, influenced by Madura and coastal trade).

  • Gudeg (Yogyakarta) — Young jackfruit slow-cooked in coconut milk and palm sugar for hours. Sweet, rich, and utterly unique. Served with rice, chicken, egg, and krecek (cow skin crackling). Try it at Gudeg Yu Djum ($2–3).
  • Nasi Liwet (Solo) — Rice cooked in coconut milk, lemongrass, and bay leaves, served with chicken, chilli, and sayur labu (chayote squash). A Solo street food staple ($1–2).
  • Pecel — Blanched vegetables (beansprouts, spinach, green beans, cucumber) with a peanut and tamarind sauce. Think of it as Indonesia's salad ($1).
  • Tahu Telur — Deep-fried tofu and egg omelette, sliced and topped with a sweet soy and peanut sauce. Crunchy, fluffy, and satisfying ($1–2).
  • Rawon (East Java) — Beef soup darkened with keluak (black nut) giving it a deep, earthy flavour. A Surabaya icon ($2–3).
  • Soto Ayam — Chicken soup with turmeric-yellow broth, vermicelli, egg, and rice cakes. Indonesia's comfort food ($1–2).

Where to try Javanese food: Yogyakarta is the best base for Central Javanese cuisine. For East Javanese, head to Surabaya or Malang. Street food centres like Malioboro Street in Yogyakarta come alive after dark with hundreds of lesehan stalls (sit-on-the-mat dining).

Sumatran Food $1–5

Sumatra's cuisine is the spiciest and most varied in Indonesia, shaped by Indian, Middle Eastern, and Chinese influences along ancient trade routes. Chillies, coconut milk, and bold spices dominate.

  • Mie Aceh — Thick yellow noodles stir-fried with beef, seafood, or goat, in a rich curry sauce with chilli, garlic, and generous amounts of curry powder. Topped with shallot crisps and served with pickled chillies. The spiciest noodle dish in Indonesia ($2–3).
  • Ikan Bakar — Grilled fish, marinated in kecap manis, chilli, and turmeric, grilled over coconut husks. Served with sambal kecap (tomato-chilli-soy dipping sauce) and steamed rice. The smoky aroma is intoxicating ($2–4).
  • Papeda (Maluku & Papua) — A sago porridge with a gluey, stretchy texture, served with yellow fish soup (kuah kuning). An acquired taste but a cultural icon ($1–2).
  • Bika Ambon (Medan) — A sweet, spongey cake made from tapioca flour, coconut milk, and eggs. Its characteristic honeycomb texture comes from spontaneous fermentation. A perfect souvenir from Medan ($3–5 for a pan).
  • Pempek (Palembang) — Fish cake dumplings made from ground mackerel and tapioca, served with a dark, sour-sweet vinegar sauce (cuka) and cucumber noodles. A Palembang icon ($1–3).

Where to try Sumatran food: Medan is the food capital of Sumatra — try the Kawasan Kesawan area for a culinary walking tour. Padang is essential for rendang and Nasi Padang. Palembang is the home of pempek.

Street Food Tips — Eat Safe, Eat Well

Indonesian street food is safe, delicious, and incredibly cheap — if you follow a few commonsense rules. Here's how to eat like a local without getting sick:

  • Eat where the locals eat. A busy warung with a queue of locals is your safest bet. High turnover means fresh ingredients. Empty stalls = red flag.
  • Watch them cook. Street food is made in front of you. If you see the meat is cooked through and the stall is clean, you're good. Avoid pre-cooked food that's been sitting out.
  • What is a "warung"? A small family-run eatery, often a cart or a few tables on the sidewalk. Warungs serve home-style cooking at half the price of restaurants. Warung tegal (warteg) is the cheapest option — rice with a selection of pre-cooked dishes.
  • Bargaining tips. Street food prices are usually fixed. If you're at a market buying fruit, a friendly smile and light bargaining ("tambah sedikit, dong" = "add a little, please") goes a long way. Don't bargain over $0.50 — it's not worth it.
  • Spice management. If you want less chilli, say "kurang pedas" (less spicy). If you want it spicy, say "tambah pedas" (more spicy). Sambal is often served on the side — add gradually.
  • Water safety. Never drink tap water. Stick to bottled water (air mineral) which costs $0.20–0.50. Ice in restaurants is usually factory-made and safe. Avoid ice from street carts unless it's clearly bagged.
  • Hand washing. Many warungs have a kobokan (finger bowl with water and lime) for washing your right hand before eating. Use it.

Essential Indonesian Phrases

  • "Satu porsi, nasi putih" — One portion with white rice
  • "Tidak pakai MSG" — No MSG, please
  • "Bungkus" — Takeaway / to go
  • "Enak sekali!" — Very delicious! (your vendor will love you)
  • "Berapa?" — How much?

Desserts & Drinks $0.50–3

Indonesian sweets and drinks are refreshing, indulgent, and often surprising. Here's what to look out for:

Desserts

  • Es Campur — Shaved ice with condensed milk, coconut jelly, grass jelly, jackfruit, avocado, and tapioca pearls. A riot of textures and sweetness. The ultimate tropical refresher ($1).
  • Martabak — Two styles: Martabak manis (sweet, thick pancake filled with chocolate, cheese, peanuts, and condensed milk) and Martabak telur (savoury, crispy folded crepe with egg, meat, and vegetables). Both are legendary ($1–3).
  • Pisang Goreng — Deep-fried banana fritters. Simple, golden, and addictive. Served with chocolate sauce, cheese, or just a dusting of sugar ($0.50–1).
  • Klepon — Glutinous rice balls filled with palm sugar, boiled, and rolled in shredded coconut. Bite carefully — the molten sugar inside is hot ($0.50).
  • Es Teler — Another shaved ice dessert, but with avocado, coconut, jackfruit, and condensed milk. Creamier than es campur ($1–2).

Drinks

  • Es Teh Manis — Iced sweet tea. The national drink. Served everywhere, always sweetened with sugar syrup. Costs about $0.20–0.50.
  • Kopi Luwak — The world's most expensive coffee, made from beans eaten and excreted by the Asian palm civet. Ethical concerns exist (caged civets), so look for wild-sourced luwak coffee. A cup costs $3–8.
  • Kopi Tubruk — Traditional Javanese coffee: coarse grounds boiled with sugar, served unfiltered. Strong, gritty, and wonderful ($0.50–1).
  • Es Jeruk — Freshly squeezed orange juice, often served extra sweet with a hint of salt. Refreshing after spicy food ($0.50–1).
  • Bandrek (West Java) — A hot ginger drink with palm sugar, cinnamon, and cloves. Perfect for rainy highland evenings in Bandung ($0.50–1).
  • Jamu — Traditional herbal medicine drinks. Jamu kunyit (turmeric) is the most common — bright yellow, earthy, and good for digestion ($0.30–0.50).

Final Tips for Eating in Indonesia

Indonesian food is best enjoyed with an open mind and an empty stomach. A few last things to keep in mind:

  • Halal is the norm. Most Indonesian food is halal (no pork, no alcohol). In Bali and Chinese-Indonesian restaurants, pork is common. Always ask if unsure.
  • Eat with your hands. In traditional settings, eating with your right hand (makan pakai tangan) is the norm. Use the thumb and fingertips to form a small rice ball — it takes practice but adds to the experience.
  • Don't skip the sambal. Every region has its own sambal. Sambal terasi (with shrimp paste) is the most iconic. Try them all.
  • Bring wet wipes. Street food is messy. A pack of wet wipes will be your best friend.
  • Be adventurous. Try durian (the king of fruits), jengkol (stinky bean), and sate kerang (clam satay). Not everything will be to your taste, but you'll leave with unforgettable memories.

Estimated Daily Food Budget

  • Street food / warung: $5–10 per day (3 meals + snacks + drinks)
  • Mid-range restaurants: $10–20 per day
  • High-end / resort dining: $25–50+ per day
  • Bottom line: You can eat incredibly well on $8–12/day
